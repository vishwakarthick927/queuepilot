QueuePilot: the Flood Twin

An AI that knows which streets will go under two hours before they do, and gets every family out on roads that will still be dry, to shelters that won't overflow.

The problem

  • Every monsoon, Indian cities flood: Chennai 2015, Bengaluru 2022, many more. Streets turn into rivers within an hour.
  • Today we react: people leave when water is already at the door. By then the roads out are flooding too, and people get stuck.
  • Cities do have flood maps, but they assume the drains work. Many are clogged with garbage and silt, so the real flooding is worse and faster.
  • Shelters get swamped all at once, while families wait outside in the rain.

The big idea: one law for everything that flows

We started with queues (people waiting at counters). A queue follows one simple law: what's waiting = what came in − what went out. Water in a street follows the same law. So do people leaving a neighbourhood, and patients in a hospital. QueuePilot is one engine for all of them. The flood twin connects three of them at once:

The brain: physics + deep learning (the research paper's method)

Plain AI learns patterns but breaks on places it hasn't seen, and can predict things that are physically impossible. Plain physics is reliable but assumes perfect drains. We combine them as the paper suggests: the neural network is only allowed to adjust one thing, how well each drain works, and only between 0% and 100%. So every forecast still obeys conservation of water. We train it through the physics (the network learns by running the water model forward).

The research gap we fill

Paper: Rose Yu & Rui Wang, "Learning dynamical systems from data: An introduction to physics-guided deep learning", PNAS 2024.

Open problem the paper listsIn simple wordsWhat we show
Generalisation (§4A)AI fails on places it never sawA district never seen in training: ours 94% caught with 4% false alarms; plain AI 85% false alarms.
Stability (§4B)No guarantee the AI's answer is physically possibleThe AI only sets drain efficiency between 0 and 1. Water is conserved by design.
Explainability (§3A)The learned part is a black boxIts output is a map of clogged drains, which an engineer can go and check.
Uncertainty (§4C)AI is over-confidentNext step: run many rain scenarios and give a flood range per street.
The big gap: the paper models nature on its own: fluids, climate, lakes. It does not cover connecting a physical system to the human systems it creates: who must leave, by which road, into which queue. We couple water → people → queues in one model, with one law.

Proof it works on real data, and it's not niche

Real data: the same physics + AI idea, applied to 300,000 real calls from a bank call centre (Technion "Anonymous Bank" dataset), learned that callers hang up at 0.14–0.15 per minute. The real measured rate is 0.156. It learned real human patience, and we can read it. Honest note: a plain data model is a bit more accurate on raw numbers there (2.73 vs 3.09).

Same engine, other places it runs today (in the repo): city emergency care (ambulances to the hospital that treats them first, with an AI radar 30 minutes ahead), EV charging hubs, perishable goods (milk, concrete), banks and offices. Anything that arrives, waits and flows.

Honest limits

  • The city and storms are simulated, so we can measure everything. Next: real rainfall radar + a real ward's drain records.
  • We assume the next 3 hours of rain are known (weather radars give this as a "nowcast"; it is not perfect).
  • The water physics is simplified (5-minute steps, 500 m cells). Good enough to decide who leaves when, not to design a drain.

Cheat sheet for judges' questions

What is it, in one line? An AI twin of a city in a flood: it predicts which streets go under 2 hours early and gets people out on dry roads to shelters that aren't full.

Where are queues in this? Queues are where we started. Shelters, intake desks, hospitals are queues. We found that water and crowds follow the same law, so one engine handles all of them.

What is the research gap? The paper models nature alone. We connect a physical system (water) to the human queues it causes, and we hit its open problems: unseen places, physically possible answers, explainability.

Why not just AI? On a district it never saw, plain AI raised false alarms 85% of the time. Nobody evacuates on that.

Why not just physics? Physics assumes clean drains. In our test, evacuating on the physics forecast still left 42,100 people cut off.

Where is the graph theory? The city's roads are a graph. Each convoy's route is a shortest path that skips roads predicted to flood before it gets there. Next: max-flow to find the bridges that must stay open.

Who would use it? City disaster-management cells, municipal corporations, state disaster response forces. On normal days the same engine runs hospitals, banks and offices.
