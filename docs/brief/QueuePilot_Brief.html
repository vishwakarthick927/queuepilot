QueuePilot: Golden Hour

An AI that sends every emergency patient to the hospital where a doctor will see them first, not just the one that is closest.

The problem, in one minute

  • A badly injured person has about one hour to get treatment. Doctors call this the golden hour.
  • Today an ambulance drives to the nearest hospital. Nobody checks if that hospital is already full.
  • When a bus crashes, every victim goes to the same hospital. It overflows. Hospitals ten minutes away sit half empty.
  • In COVID's second wave, families drove from hospital to hospital looking for a bed. Some died on the way.
Our idea: before the ambulance leaves, predict the waiting time at every hospital. Send the patient where drive time + waiting time is shortest.

How it works

The brain: physics + AI together

Pure AI can make impossible predictions (like a negative queue) and breaks when something new happens. Pure physics is too simple: it does not know people give up and leave, or that every hospital behaves differently. We use both, the way the research paper suggests. The AI part is only allowed to do things that are physically possible: it can slow service down or let waiting people leave, but it can never invent or delete patients.

The research paper and the gap we fill

Paper: Rose Yu & Rui Wang, "Learning dynamical systems from data: An introduction to physics-guided deep learning", PNAS 2024. It shows how to mix physics with deep learning. At the end it lists problems that are still open:

Open problem in the paperIn simple wordsWhat we do
Generalisation (§4A)AI fails on situations it never sawTested on days with fewer staff and 50% more patients than training. A new place is learned from one day of data.
Stability (§4B)No guarantee the AI's answers make senseThe AI is boxed in by physics, so every prediction is physically possible by design.
Uncertainty (§4C)AI is over-confidentWe give a range, and check that reality falls inside it about 90% of the time.
Explainability (§3A)The AI part is a black boxOur AI outputs real things: "chance a waiting person gives up per minute".
The big gap: every example in the paper is about nature: fluids, climate, lakes, drones, epidemics. Nobody has used physics-guided deep learning for systems run by people: hospitals, queues, crowds. That is what we do.
Something new we found: from queue lengths alone, "people gave up" and "service got slower" look exactly the same. The paper does not mention this. Fix: also learn from records of who left (call-centre hang-ups, "left without being seen" in hospitals).
Tested on real data: 300,000 real calls from a bank call centre (Technion SEE "Anonymous Bank" dataset). Our model learned that callers hang up at 0.14–0.15 per minute. The real, measured rate is 0.156. The AI learned real human patience, and we can read it. Honest note: a pure data model is still slightly more accurate on raw queue length (2.7 vs 3.1); our edge is trust and explainability.

One engine, many sectors

Anything where things arrive, wait and get served is the same problem: patients and doctors, cars and chargers, trucks of milk and factories. Waiting destroys something every time: lives, energy or money. QueuePilot only needs one simple table to start: arrived · started · finished (or left) · who served.

Cheat sheet for judges' questions

What is the use case? City emergency care. Send each ambulance to the hospital where treatment starts soonest. Same idea works for hospitals, crowds, EV hubs, perishable goods.

What is the research gap? Physics-guided deep learning has only been used for nature. We bring it to systems run by people, and tackle four problems the paper itself calls open: new situations, stability, uncertainty and explainability.

Why not just use AI? Plain AI can predict impossible things and breaks on new situations. In an emergency you need answers that always make sense. Physics guarantees that.

Why not just use physics? Physics does not know people give up, or that each hospital is different. The AI learns that.

Where is the graph theory? The city is a graph of incidents, roads and hospitals. Each patient is matched to a hospital by the shortest "drive + wait" time. Next step: min-cut on this graph to show the city's real bottleneck.

Is it real? The demo city is simulated so we can measure everything. The forecasting method is tested on 300,000 real bank calls.

Who pays? State ambulance services (108), hospital chains, smart-city control rooms. Every other day, the same engine cuts waiting in OPDs, banks and offices.
